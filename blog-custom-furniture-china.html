Custom Furniture from China: What to Know Before You Order

September 2026 · 7 min read · CASA TRIO

Ordering custom furniture from China is one of the best ways to get exactly what you want — but it's also very different from buying ready-made pieces online. Custom work means drawings, specifications, material choices and production coordination, and doing this across borders adds another layer of detail to manage.

Whether you're an interior designer specifying pieces for a client, or a homeowner furnishing a villa abroad, here's what you need to know before you place a custom order with a Chinese factory.

What "Custom Furniture" Actually Means in China

In the Chinese furniture market, "custom" usually covers three levels:

1. Dimension modification

Changing the size of an existing showroom model — making a sofa longer, a bed higher, a dining table wider. This is the easiest type of custom and most factories offer it. The design stays the same; only the dimensions change.

2. Material and finish customization

Keeping the design but changing the fabric, leather, wood veneer, color, hardware or leg style. This is very common in Foshan showrooms — you pick a model, then pick your materials from sample books.

3. Fully bespoke design

Building something from scratch based on your own drawings, 3D renders or designer's specifications. This includes custom cabinetry, kitchens, wardrobes, built-in storage and one-off furniture pieces. It requires more communication and a factory capable of detailed production planning.

Key Distinction

Not every factory that offers "custom" can handle fully bespoke work. Many are good at modifying existing designs but lack the engineering capability to build from drawings. Confirm what level of custom the supplier actually does before you commit.

What You Need Before You Start

The more prepared you are, the smoother production goes. Before contacting factories, have these ready:

Drawings or clear references

For dimension modifications: photos of the piece you like, plus the exact sizes you need. For fully bespoke work: 2D drawings or 3D renders with dimensions. Even hand-drawn sketches with measurements are better than trying to describe what you want in words.

Material specifications

What fabric or leather do you want? What wood finish? What color hardware? If you're working with a designer, share the material schedule. If not, be ready to review physical samples — don't rely on screen colors.

Your budget range

Custom furniture pricing varies enormously based on materials, complexity and quantity. Sharing your budget range upfront helps the supplier propose realistic options instead of quoting something you can't use.

Timeline expectations

Custom production takes longer than ready-made. Standard upholstered pieces run 30–40 days. Custom cabinetry and built-ins run 35–50 days. Factor in shipping time on top of that.

How the Custom Order Process Works

  1. Initial inquiry — Share your drawings, dimensions and material preferences. The supplier reviews and sends a quotation.
  2. Quotation review — Check that the quote includes all materials, construction details and packaging. Ask questions about anything unclear.
  3. Sample approval — Before production starts, approve physical material samples (fabric swatches, leather pieces, finish boards). This is the most important step for avoiding surprises.
  4. Deposit — Production typically starts after a 30–50% deposit. The balance is due after quality inspection and before shipment.
  5. Production — Factories send progress photos. This is when you can ask questions about materials, workmanship or construction.
  6. Pre-shipment inspection — Someone checks every piece: dimensions, materials, workmanship, function and packaging. Photos are documented.
  7. Packing and shipping — Items are packed for export, loaded into a container, and shipped to your destination.

Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

Pitfall 1: "Custom" means the factory will guess

If your drawings are unclear or your specs are vague, the factory will make decisions for you — and they might not be the decisions you want. Everything needs to be confirmed in writing: dimensions, materials, colors, hardware, construction details and finish.

Pitfall 2: Approving color on a screen

Computer screens and showroom lighting make colors look different from real life. The warm beige you see online might actually be a cooler gray in person. Always order physical material samples and approve them before production.

Pitfall 3: Not checking the construction

Two sofas that look identical can have completely different frames, suspension systems and foam density. Ask what's inside: solid wood frame or plywood? What foam density? What suspension system? These details determine how the furniture will hold up over time.

Pitfall 4: Underestimating lead time

Custom furniture takes time — and shipping takes more time. If you're furnishing a home for a move-in date or a hotel opening, build in buffer. Production delays, shipping schedules and customs clearance all add up.

Do You Need to Visit China?

Not necessarily. While visiting factories and showrooms is valuable, many custom projects are successfully managed remotely — especially when you work with a team on the ground.

What matters is having someone who can:

  • Confirm your specifications with the factory in detail
  • Arrange and review material samples
  • Track production and flag issues early
  • Conduct a physical inspection before shipment
  • Document everything with photos and measurements

If you're an overseas homeowner or interior designer, this is where a local sourcing team saves real time and reduces risk.
