How Does a Furniture Sourcing Agent in China Work?

October 2026 · 7 min read · CASA TRIO

If you're planning a furniture project and looking at suppliers in China, you've probably come across the term "sourcing agent." It sounds simple — someone who helps you buy furniture from China. But in practice, the role covers a lot more ground, and the difference between a good agent and a bad one usually shows up months later, when the container arrives.

Here's how furniture sourcing from China actually works when you work with an agent — what they do at each stage, how they get paid, and how to tell whether you need one at all.

What a sourcing agent actually does

A sourcing agent is not a supplier. They don't manufacture the furniture, and they don't own a warehouse full of products. Their job is to manage the whole buying process on your behalf, across multiple factories:

  • Understanding your project. Style, rooms, budget, timeline, project stage. The agent translates your project into a concrete buying list.
  • Finding and vetting suppliers. Most agents work with a curated network of factories they know and have visited — not every factory that happens to send a quote.
  • Comparing quotes on the same basis. Two factories can quote the same sofa at very different prices because one includes better foam, real wood, or softer-close hardware. A good agent compares specifications, not just numbers.
  • Confirming specifications in writing. Materials, dimensions, finishes, quantities — agreed before production starts, so there's no room for "we thought you meant…" later.
  • Following up on production. Checking timelines, flagging delays early, and resolving problems while the order is still in China — where they can actually be fixed.
  • Quality control before shipping. Checking the finished goods against the approved specifications before the container is loaded.
  • Coordinating export packing and logistics. Export packaging, container loading, shipping documents, and handover to your freight forwarder.
What an agent doesn't do

An agent doesn't guarantee duty-free delivery, doesn't handle customs clearance in your country (unless separately agreed), and can't promise a fixed delivery date. Anyone who promises all of that on a furniture order should be asked exactly how.

If this sounds like a lot of steps — it is. That's the point. Buying from overseas works best when each step has a clear owner, and the owner is on the ground where the furniture is actually made. Sourcing agent vs. furniture supplier

The difference matters, especially for designers and developers:

  • A furniture supplier sells you their own products — what they make, in their own style and price range.
  • A sourcing agent organizes selection, purchasing, production follow-up, QC and logistics across a network of vetted suppliers — so you're not limited to one factory's range.

Both models work. A supplier makes sense when you've already found the exact factory you want. An agent makes sense when your project spans multiple rooms, styles, or product categories — which is most residential and commercial projects.

How sourcing agents get paid

This is the question clients should always ask early, because the fee structure tells you a lot about how an agent works. There are two common models:

  • Residential projects (product pricing). The client pays a product price for the furniture, and the agent's margin is built into that price. No separate procurement fee is charged. Design support, if needed, is quoted separately.
  • B2B projects (transparent cost + agency fee). The client sees the supplier cost, pays an agency fee, and covers shipping. The fee structure is confirmed per project before work starts.

Whichever model applies, the important thing is that it's confirmed in writing before you commit — what's included, what's extra, and who pays for what. If an agent is vague about fees at the start, that vagueness usually returns later in the project.

Before you pay anything

Ask for a short written scope: which rooms or product categories are included, what deliverables you'll receive at each stage, and how the deposit or initiation fee is applied. This protects both sides and makes the project much easier to manage.

How to choose a sourcing agent

Here are the questions worth asking before you commit to anyone:

  1. How many suppliers do you actually work with, and how do you choose them? The answer should be specific — a curated network, factories you've visited — not "we know everyone."
  2. How do you get paid, exactly? Product pricing, or cost plus agency fee? Confirm it in writing.
  3. Who does the quality inspection? Do they check the goods themselves before loading, and what happens if something doesn't match the approved specifications?
  4. How do you handle changes and problems? Delays, material substitutions and price changes happen. Who decides, and how are you informed?
  5. What happens if I don't like the first options? Make sure there's a clear feedback and revision path before you pay a deposit.

When you probably don't need an agent

An agent isn't always necessary:

  • You're buying a single item or a small standard order, and you already know the factory.
  • You have a local team in China handling sourcing and QC.
  • You're buying from a marketplace where the product is fully standardized and already inspected by the platform.

For anything multi-room, multi-category, or tied to a design concept — a villa, a whole home, a hotel, a showroom — the coordination an agent provides usually saves more than it costs.

Frequently asked questions

Is a sourcing agent the same as a trading company?

Not exactly. A trading company resells goods and may represent just a few factories. A sourcing agent works across multiple vetted suppliers on your behalf, and is accountable for the coordination between them.

Do I still get to choose the products?

Yes. The agent shortlists options that match your style, budget and specifications — you make the final choices. The agent's job is to make sure what you chose is what gets produced and shipped.
